4.6.1 Definitions of employment, unemployment and full employment

  • Employment: Refers to members of the working-age population who are actively engaged in paid work, either as employees or self-employed individuals.
  • Unemployment: Occurs when individuals who are of working age, able and willing to work, and actively seeking employment are unable to find a job.
  • Full employment: The level of employment where all available labor resources are used efficiently, meaning cyclical unemployment is zero and only unavoidable frictional or structural joblessness remains.

4.6.2 Measurement of unemployment

  • Labour Force Survey (LFS): A standard survey-based method used to gather statistics on the proportion of the economically active population that is currently unemployed.
  • Unemployment rate formula: Unemployment Rate (%) = (Number of Unemployed People / Total Labour Force) × 100.

4.6.3 Causes/types of unemployment

  • Frictional unemployment: Short-term unemployment experienced by individuals who are between jobs or entering the labour market.

Frictional Unemployment

  • Structural unemployment: Long-term unemployment caused by structural shifts in the economy, technological advances, or international competition causing a mismatch between worker skills and available job roles.

Understanding Structural Unemployment in One Minute: Definition, Explanation, Examples and Causes

  • Cyclical unemployment: Unemployment resulting from a lack of aggregate demand during economic downturns or recessions.

Cyclical Unemployment

  • Seasonal unemployment: Occurs when demand for workers fluctuates depending on specific times of the year, such as tourism, agriculture, or retail seasons.

4.6.4 Consequences of unemployment

  • For individuals: Lower personal income, reduced standard of living, loss of work skills, and increased mental stress or social isolation.
  • For producers/firms: Reduced consumer spending leads to lower sales revenue and profits, though firms may benefit from a larger pool of job applicants.
  • For the government: Decreased tax revenue (from income tax and spending taxes) combined with increased expenditure on unemployment welfare payments.
  • For the economy: Underutilisation of economic productive capacity, resulting in lower national real output (GDP) and wasted human resources.

4.6.5 Policies to reduce unemployment

  • Demand-side policies: Expansionary fiscal policy (tax cuts, public spending) and expansionary monetary policy (lower interest rates) stimulate aggregate demand to reduce cyclical unemployment, though they can risk higher inflation or budget deficits.
  • Supply-side policies: Education, job retraining programs, occupational mobility grants, and labour market deregulation reduce structural and frictional unemployment effectively in the long term, though they can be costly and time-consuming.
