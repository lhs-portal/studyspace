Introduction to Fiscal Policy

4.2.1 Government budget

  • Government budget: A financial plan stating the government's expected revenues (primarily from taxation) and spending over a specific period (usually one year).
  • Government budget deficit: Occurs when annual government expenditure exceeds annual revenue (government spending > tax revenue).

Budget Deficits (Deficit Spending) and Surpluses Defined, Explained & Compared in One Minute

  • Government budget surplus: Occurs when annual government revenue exceeds annual expenditure (tax revenue > government spending).
  • Calculation: Budget Position = Total Government Revenue − Total Government Spending. A positive value indicates a surplus, while a negative value indicates a deficit.

4.2.2 Reasons for government spending

  • Main areas of spending: include public services (healthcare and education), public and merit goods, infrastructure (transport and energy), welfare transfers (pensions and unemployment benefits), defence and public safety, and debt interest payments.
  • Reasons and effects: To supply public and merit goods, boost total demand during economic downturns, reduce income inequality via welfare transfers, and enhance national productivity through long-term infrastructure investment.

Fiscal Policy and Crowding Out

4.2.3 Taxation

  • Reasons for taxation: raising revenue for government spending, discouraging the consumption of demerit goods, reducing imports to protect domestic industries, redistributing income to lower inequality, influencing total macroeconomic demand, and encouraging environmental sustainability.

Are Taxes Fair? The Redistribution of Wealth via Taxation Explained in One Minute

  • Classifications of tax:
  • Progressive tax: Takes a higher percentage of income as income increases (e.g., income tax).
  • Regressive tax: Takes a lower percentage of income as income increases (e.g., sales tax or VAT).
  • Proportional tax: Takes the same percentage of income across all income levels.
  • Direct tax: Levied directly on income or wealth (e.g., personal income tax, corporation tax).
  • Indirect tax: levied on goods and services, paid via suppliers (e.g., GST/VAT, excise duties).
  • Impact of taxation: Affects disposable income and spending power for consumers, work incentives for workers, production costs and profit margins for producers/firms, total revenue collected by the government, and total demand and resource allocation in the economy.

4.2.4 Definition of fiscal policy

  • Fiscal policy refers to the decisions made by the government regarding taxation and public spending to influence total demand and economic activity and achieve macroeconomic aims.

4.2.5 Fiscal policy measures

  • Expansionary fiscal policy: Involves lowering direct or indirect tax rates and/or increasing government spending to stimulate aggregate demand during economic downturns.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy: Involves raising tax rates and/or reducing government spending to decrease aggregate demand and slow down demand-pull inflation.

4.2.6 Effects of fiscal policy on government macroeconomic aims

  • Economic growth & Employment: Expansionary fiscal policy increases consumer disposable income and government spending, expanding real GDP and boosting demand for labour to reduce cyclical unemployment.
  • Price stability: Contractionary fiscal policy reduces total demand to control demand-pull inflation and maintain stable prices.
  • Redistribution of income: Progressive direct taxes combined with increased welfare spending redistribute wealth and lower poverty.
  • Environmental sustainability & balance of payments: Higher tariffs and indirect taxes on demerit goods encourage green choices and regulate import demand.
