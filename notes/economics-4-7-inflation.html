4.7.1 Definitions of inflation and deflation

  • Inflation: A sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time, leading to a fall in the purchasing power of money.

Inflation Explained in One Minute

  • Deflation: A sustained decrease in the general price level of goods and services in an economy, causing the purchasing power of money to rise.

Deflation Explained in One Minute

4.7.2 Measurement of inflation

  • Consumer Prices Index (CPI): The primary metric used to measure inflation by tracking price changes for a representative "basket of goods and services" consumed by average households.

Understanding Inflation and CPI (Consumer Price Index)

  • CPI Measurement Process:
  • Selecting the Basket: A selection of commonly purchased goods and services based on household expenditure surveys.
  • Weighting: Assigning weights to items based on their proportion of total household income spent, ensuring price changes in major categories (e.g., housing, food) have a greater impact on the overall index.
  • Base Year & Index Calculation: Comparing the current cost of the weighted basket against a chosen base year (set at an index value of 100) to calculate the percentage inflation rate over time.

4.7.3 Causes of inflation

  • Demand-pull inflation: Occurs when aggregate demand for goods and services outpaces aggregate supply, caused by factors such as excessive money supply growth, lower interest rates, increased consumer expenditure, or expansionary fiscal spending.
  • Cost-push inflation: Occurs when production costs rise for firms, forcing them to increase prices to maintain profit margins. Caused by rising raw material prices, higher wages, increased indirect taxation, or imported inflation due to currency depreciation.

4.7.4 Consequences of inflation

  • Savers: Lose purchasing power if nominal interest rates on savings accounts are lower than the inflation rate.
  • Lenders: Lose money because the real value of future debt repayments is reduced by inflation.
  • Borrowers: Benefit because they repay loans in the future using money that has less purchasing power than when originally borrowed.
  • Consumers: Experience a fall in real purchasing power unless wages increase at or above the rate of inflation.
  • Workers: May demand higher wages to keep up with living costs, potentially triggering a wage-price spiral.
  • Producers/Firms: Face menu costs (updating price lists) and shoe-leather costs, while heightened uncertainty hampers long-term investment planning.
  • The economy suffers reduced international competitiveness as exports become relatively more expensive, potentially worsening the current account balance.

4.7.5 Policies to control inflation

  • Contractionary monetary policy: Raising interest rates or restricting money supply to reduce borrowing and consumer spending, directly curbing demand-pull inflation.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy: Increasing direct/indirect taxes or cutting government spending to reduce total demand in the economy.
  • Supply-side policies: Improving labour productivity, education, and infrastructure to increase productive capacity, reducing cost-push inflation in the long term.
