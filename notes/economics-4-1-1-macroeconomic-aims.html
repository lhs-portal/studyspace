Economics ? Government and the Macroeconomy

4.1.1 Macroeconomic aims

The Six Macroeconomic Aims

  • Economic growth: an increase in the output of an economy in the short run and an increase in the economy?s productive potential in the long run

    • Short-run economic growth is called actual economic growth.

    Long-run economic growth can be achieved as a result of a rise in the quantity and quality of factors of production. This is called potential economic growth, which is an increase in an economy?s productive capacity.

    The difference between actual economic growth and potential economic growth can be shown on a production possibility curve (PPC) diagram.

    Here, movement from point A to point B represents actual economic growth ? meaning more capital and consumer goods are made. The outward shift from YY to ZZ represents potential economic growth ? meaning the economy is capable of producing more.

    Economists use Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply to explain economic growth.

    Aggregate Demand (AD) is the total demand for a country?s products at a given price level. It consists of consumer expenditure, investment, government spending, and net exports.

    Formula: C+I+G+(X-M)

    AD increases when there is an increase in population, a decrease in interest rate, a decrease in exchange rate, and an increase in confidence.

    Aggregate Demand (AS) is the total amount of goods and services domestic firms are willing and able to produce at a given price level.

    AS is perfectly elastic if the economy has unemployed resources. It changes to perfectly inelastic when all resources are employed or when the economy reaches full employment. This increases costs and prices.

    When aggregate supply increases, the curve shifts to the right. This could happen as a result of increased quantity and quality of resources and decreased costs.

    How much growth is expected?

    It depends on the level of output in relation to its current maximum possible output and its growth in productive capacity.

    This means if the economy is 2% below maximum possible output and productive capacity, and production capacity is expected to increase by 3%, the expected economic growth rate is 5%.

  • Full employment / Low unemployment:

    • Full employment is the lowest employment possible. This means people who are willing to work at the going wage rate can find employment when there is full employment.

    Full employment can ensure that all available labour resources are actively utilised, minimising cyclical and structural joblessness to maximise economic productive capacity.

    People who are not willing to work are called economically inactive. These dependents who are not in the labour force include children, the elderly, students in full-time education, homemakers and those who are sick and disabled.

    People who are working or unemployed people who are actively seeking work are in the labour force and are economically active.

    How much is the target employment?

    Full employment is not possible. This is because even if demand for labour is equal to supply of labour, there are still people changing jobs and being unemployed for short periods of time.

    So, governments expect a low unemployment rate. Most countries are set to achieve unemployment near to 3%.

  • Stable prices / Low inflation:

    • Stable prices mean the price level in the economy is not changing significantly over time.

    Inflation means a rise in the average price level of goods and services over time.

    Inflation rate measures the percentage rise in the price level of goods and services over time.

    Governments aim to keep the general price level increases predictable and low (typically around 2%) to safeguard purchasing power, maintain business confidence, and prevent real wage erosion.

  • Balance of payments stability: Maintaining equilibrium in trade flows so foreign earnings cover import expenditure, preventing severe current account deficits or currency instability.
  • Redistribution of income: Reducing income inequality through progressive taxation, welfare transfers, and direct provision of essential public services to alleviate poverty.
  • Environmental sustainability: Promoting economic development that satisfies current needs without depleting natural resources or degrading ecosystems for future generations.

    • 4.1.2 Reasons Behind the Choice of Aims and Criteria

  • Reasons for Government Choice: Governments prioritise aims based on their current economic stage, social needs, voter priorities, and international commitments. For example, developing economies may prioritise growth and job creation, whereas advanced economies may prioritise low inflation and environmental sustainability.
  • Criteria for Setting Targets: Governments establish quantitative indicators (e.g., target GDP growth rates, CPI inflation targets around 2%, or target unemployment rates) to evaluate macroeconomic performance and adjust fiscal or monetary interventions effectively.

    • 4.1.3 Potential Conflicts Between Macroeconomic Aims

  • Full employment vs. stable prices: As unemployment falls and economic output expands, increased consumer expenditure and wage pressures can induce demand-pull and cost-push inflation.
  • Economic growth vs. environmental sustainability: Rapid expansion of industrial output often results in increased resource consumption, pollution, deforestation, and greenhouse gas emissions, threatening long-term environmental sustainability.
  • Full employment vs. balance of payments stability: Higher employment levels elevate household incomes, leading to increased demand for imported goods and services, which can widen a current account deficit.
